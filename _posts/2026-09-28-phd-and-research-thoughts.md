It has been a while since I finished my PhD. I wanted to write down a few things I have been thinking about—not as universal advice, but as notes for people who might recognize themselves in this path.

## 1. Start. Try your best to start.

The first project is always the hardest. Use the resources you have and reach out to anyone who can help you. Learn, explore, study as much as you can, and try to find an entry point.

Remember that **a rough start is a good start. This is always true.** Interestingly, though, the way someone starts can already reveal a lot about their future research trajectory—their interests, style, taste, and judgment. Even when the work itself is rough, the choices you make at the beginning often say something about the kind of researcher you will become.

## 2. Be broad.

In my opinion, there are a lot of low-hanging fruits in almost every field, but sometimes experts fail to notice them because they are too narrow.

When you explore many different fields, it becomes much easier to identify interesting entry points. You can connect ideas from different areas, do a lot of "A + B," and produce fairly solid work that can often be accepted by top conferences.

Breadth gives you the ability to see connections that people deeply embedded in a single field may overlook.

## 3. Be paced.

I used to be crazy about research and work. They were almost the entirety of my focus. Eventually, I realized that this was hurting both my cognitive capabilities and my overall health.

I would suggest prioritizing your overall happiness above everything else. The earlier you realize that this is ultimately the goal, the earlier you get onto the right track in life.

In other words, go inward and connect with yourself. Changing your environment or external circumstances is rarely the ultimate answer. Achieving something will not automatically make you happy.

## 4. Learn and grow with collaborators.

The more high-quality collaborators you have, the better your research will become.

This is difficult at the beginning. When you first start doing research, you may only have access to your advisors and labmates. Hopefully, through internships, conferences, and random networking opportunities, you gradually meet more collaborators.

Be genuinely open-minded. Working with different people exposes you to different ways of thinking, and interacting with new collaborators can help you maintain that openness.

## 5. Get resources.

People and collaborators are enormous resources. So are internship opportunities, APIs, GPUs, infrastructure, and many other things.

I have always tried to put myself in places where I can have more resources and more flexibility. And yes, flexibility itself, in my opinion, is an extremely valuable resource.

In some sense:

**Success = people + resources + flexibility.**

## 6. Breadth first, depth second.

In my personal opinion, breadth comes first, and depth comes second.

I admire great researchers who dive deeply into a certain direction and eventually make important findings. But I think it is very hard to do this at the very beginning, because you often do not yet know which direction deserves that level of commitment.

When I look at researchers I admire, I notice that many of them spent some time working on things that, in retrospect, were not that important. Their trajectories were not perfectly clean. They explored, tried different problems, and sometimes worked on things that did not end up defining them.

Then, at some point, they found the right track.

They found an entry point—one problem, one direction, one perspective, or one domain—that connected with the broader context of the world. They gave it a serious shot, went much deeper than everyone else, and sometimes impressed the world.

So to me, breadth and depth are not contradictory. **Breadth helps you search for the right place to go deep.**

You need enough exposure to understand what is happening in the world: what people already know, what people care about, what has already been tried, what is becoming possible, and what people might still be missing. Then, eventually, you find a direction where you believe you can contribute something meaningful.

Once you find it, depth starts to matter much more.

**But when it comes to an industry job, depth starts to matter much more.** You may need to concentrate most of your attention on a single direction. In that environment, being extremely broad can sometimes become a distraction.

You probably should not constantly be distracted by the breadth of the research community. Instead, focus deeply on one direction and build your own **moat**—a body of expertise, context, and accumulated understanding that is difficult for others to replicate quickly.

That accumulated knowledge and experience is what makes it difficult for another smart person, even someone who learns and moves very quickly, to immediately replace what you can do.

I used to call this *experience*.

But increasingly, I feel that experience itself may matter less than it used to, because AI can give you information, explain unfamiliar topics, and teach you almost anything remarkably quickly.

What may matter more is your **profile**: the body of work, reputation, relationships, positioning, and identity that other people associate with you.

And that is a very human thing.

To some extent, I even think it is a little silly. But this is how society works.

There is a connection between depth and profile. If you stay in one direction long enough, people gradually start to associate you with certain problems, ideas, and capabilities. In a career, that association matters. It helps other people understand what you are good at and why they should come to you.

But I do not think building a profile is the ultimate goal.

The ultimate goal is still to **expand the world's knowledge**.

You want to make people aware of something they have missed. You want to reveal a perspective they have not seen before. You want to make something work much better, or make an idea substantially more effective and useful.

That is why depth matters to me.

Not because being specialized is inherently good. Not because having more years of experience is inherently valuable. And not because you simply want to make yourself harder to replace.

Depth matters because, once you find the right entry point, it gives you the chance to push far enough that you can contribute something genuinely new to the world.

So my view is: **explore broadly first, find your entry point, and then go deep.**

## 7. A personal reflection on my PhD.

When we are young researchers, we should have the ability—and maybe even the appetite—to absorb new things as they appear. New ideas, new problems, new methods, new fields, new trends. We should consume as much as we can, digest as much as we can, and try to understand how everything connects.

I genuinely enjoy exploring. And I still believe that this instinct is good not only for research, but also for life. Exploration gives you more perspectives, more possibilities, and more ways of understanding the world.

At the same time, I do not really call myself an expert yet.

I still feel confused when solving many problems, even within the areas I have worked in. Part of the reason is that the "field" I have involved myself in has become too broad. I have touched many things, but I have not yet gone deep enough into one place to feel that I completely own it.

Sometimes I notice that truly exceptional researchers seem to complete this breadth-to-depth process very early. They explore quickly, understand the landscape quickly, find their direction quickly, and then go deep. In some sense, the earlier someone can complete this transition, the more extraordinary they seem to me.

I am still on my way.
